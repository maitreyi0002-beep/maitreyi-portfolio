About the project

Loop is an AI-powered restaurant operations platform. It brings together data from across a restaurant's systems and uses AI agents to help teams understand what’s happening, diagnose problems, and take action.

Imagine walking into a grocery store, asking a clerk for bread, and being told: “Sorry, this is the vegetable aisle. Go outside, guess which door leads to the bakery, and ask again.” That was Loop’s AI experience.

Understanding the problem

The Wrong-Door Problem

We had built four capable, task-specific AI agents: Loop Chat for broad questions, Loop Charts for dashboards, Loop Voice for scheduled verbal debriefs, and Loop Sheets for financial modelling. Each had its own front door.

People do not arrive thinking about system architecture. They arrive with something to get done. Yet we had quietly accumulated eight separate entry points across the platform.

What the telemetry showed
  • 6% of sessions on the landing agent were out-of-scope requests that it refused.
  • People moved from Charts to Chat 51 times, versus 30 in the other direction.
  • Users made a best guess, got rejected, then had to hunt for another door.

A customer wanted to edit an ad-spend plan. She asked the landing agent, then manually switched to Chat, and was rejected again. The capability existed; it was hiding behind the wrong door.

Uncovering the real behavior

Before you can fix human behavior, you have to filter out the ghosts.

I started with three months of telemetry: 1,776 interactive sessions across 473 real users. The first discovery was about the data itself.

The 49% ghost traffic

Nearly half of the session log had no pageviews, cursor movements, or other human signal. These were automated report runs and internal evaluation scripts. After cross-referencing timestamps with browser activity in PostHog, I removed that traffic and returned to the human evidence.

Assumptions that didn’t survive the cleanup

We thought the funnel was leaking.
Reach was flat at 20 to 24% of monthly users, while 48% of people who completed an agent session returned on two or more days. Access was the problem.
We thought AI reliability was failing.
Technical errors accounted for 1% of session deaths. Unanswered clarifying questions accounted for 17%, and flat no-data refusals for 13%.
We thought people ignored questions.
Median turn latency was 74 seconds. In 44% of unanswered clarifying questions, the person had already left before the prompt rendered.

61% of people who hit a soft dead-end did not return within 24 hours. It was less an intelligence problem than a product that failed to get out of its own way.

Fixing the visual identity crisis

Moving from four separate dialects to a single, atmospheric design language.

Four agents had been built by four teams at four points in time. Chat felt like a sparse messaging app. Charts looked like the main landing page. Sheets had gone full retro spreadsheet. They spoke to the same person, but did not look like the same product layer.

  1. Atmosphere over containersMove from utility-heavy grey cards toward glass, depth, and the Powder Steel palette.
  2. Data as the heroLet information take centre stage instead of wrapping it in UI layers.
  3. A brand that reactsTurn the Loop mark into a dynamic status light that can breathe, shimmer, and settle.

Designing for two audiences: humans + LLMs

For an agentic interface, the design system is not only a library for people. It is a set of constraints that helps an AI make consistent decisions. The system had 8 foundations, 17 primitives, and 14 compositions, documented as specific render rules an LLM could follow.

The architecture call: Super Agent

Scope should be the platform’s problem, not the user’s homework.

We considered silently routing prompts while keeping separate interfaces, or consolidating everything into a single front door. We chose consolidation.

ONE FRONT DOORThe user states the goal; the platform finds capability.↓Intent classification
Metric lookup & diagnosis · Reports & charts · Financial projections

One conversational input classifies the underlying intent: metric lookup, report generation, financial analysis, plan edits, or diagnosis, and dispatches it to the right capability. The person describes their goal; the platform handles scope.

Designing for human friction

Turning the moments where people gave up into something the interface could actually fix.

Keep the work where you can see it

Friction: AI-generated work disappeared into the conversation. Idea: a persistent split canvas kept the conversation on the left and artifacts on the right, so the chat could keep moving while the work stayed put.

Make waiting feel honest

Friction: a 74-second average response left people staring at a spinner. Idea: the Loop Log showed the agent’s live steps, from resolving data sources to ranking insights, including timing and inspectable SQL.

Don’t make people type what they could tap

Friction: open-ended clarification questions caused 17% of session deaths. Idea: when Super Agent was nearly certain, it offered single-tap Clarification Cards for stores, dates, and platforms. They were always skippable; the composer was never blocked.

Put the interesting question within reach

Friction: diagnosis appeared in only 2% of first queries, even though it was a flagship capability. Idea: every metric result included a “Why did this change?” suggestion so one tap could turn a passive number into an investigation.

And then it started looking like one product

What I took away

  1. Data hygiene is design work. Before designing for behavior, make sure you are looking at human behavior.
  2. The best AI interfaces don’t make users work like AI. The more the system understands intent, the less people should have to understand the system.
  3. Designing for AI made us better at designing for humans. When machines became consumers of the design system, ambiguity stopped being good enough.
